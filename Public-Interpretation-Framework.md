# Public Interpretation Framework v1.0

**Version:** 1.0  

---

# 1. Purpose

The Public Interpretation Framework defines how the Stokely Historical Preservation system communicates historical understanding to audiences.

Public interpretation answers the question:

> How do we help people understand history while preserving accuracy, context, and trust?

This framework supports:

- Museum exhibitions
- Digital archives
- Books
- Documentaries
- Educational materials
- Public websites
- Interactive experiences

---

# 2. Public Interpretation Philosophy

## 2.1 Interpretation Is a Translation Process

The internal preservation system contains:

- Detailed evidence
- Metadata
- Provenance
- Relationships
- Historical analysis

Public interpretation translates this complexity into understandable experiences.

It must preserve the essential meaning without overwhelming the audience.

---

## 2.2 Accuracy Comes Before Engagement

Historical interpretation should be:

- Interesting
- Accessible
- Meaningful

But never at the expense of:

- Accuracy
- Context
- Evidence
- Transparency

---

## 2.3 Audiences Deserve Honest History

Public interpretation should distinguish between:

- Documented facts
- Historical interpretations
- Personal experiences
- Areas of uncertainty

Trust is built through transparency.

---

# 3. Interpretation Layers

Public interpretation should operate at multiple levels.

---

# 3.1 Object Level

Focus:

"What is this item?"

Examples:

- Photograph
- Artifact
- Document
- Video

Includes:

- Description
- Provenance
- Historical relevance

---

# 3.2 Story Level

Focus:

"What happened?"

Uses:

- Narrative episodes
- Historical assertions
- Evidence

---

# 3.3 Theme Level

Focus:

"Why does this matter?"

Uses:

- Historical themes
- Broader context
- Connections across history

---

# 3.4 Meaning Level

Focus:

"Why should audiences care?"

Connects history to:

- Human experience
- Cultural significance
- Continuing impact

---

# 4. Public Interpretation Standards

---

## 4.1 Evidence-Based Presentation

Public materials should connect to:

- Supporting resources
- Historical assertions
- Approved narratives

---

## 4.2 Context Preservation

Information should be presented with sufficient context.

Avoid:

- Isolated facts
- Misleading summaries
- Oversimplified conclusions

---

## 4.3 Perspective Transparency

When presenting personal experiences:

Identify them appropriately.

Examples:

"Scott Stokely recalls..."

rather than:

"Everyone believed..."

---

## 4.4 Appropriate Uncertainty

Unknown or debated information should remain identified.

Examples:

- "According to..."
- "Available evidence suggests..."
- "The exact circumstances remain unclear."

---

# 6. Audience Considerations

Public interpretation should consider:

- Prior knowledge
- Accessibility needs
- Cultural context
- Learning goals

A single historical collection may serve:

- Casual visitors
- Researchers
- Students
- Community members

---

# 7. Accessibility Standards

Interpretation should support:

- Clear language
- Multiple formats
- Assistive technologies
- Different learning styles

Accessibility expands participation in history.

---

# 8. Public Interpretation and AI

Future AI systems may assist with:

- Audience-specific summaries
- Interactive exploration
- Translation
- Discovery tools

However:

AI-generated public interpretation must remain connected to:

- Approved assertions
- Evidence
- Rights information

AI must not create unsupported historical narratives.

---

# 9. Interpretation Integrity Rules

## Rule 1

Public materials must be traceable to institutional knowledge.

---

## Rule 2

Engagement must not override accuracy.

---

## Rule 3

Personal stories must remain identified as personal perspectives.

---

## Rule 4

Uncertainty must not be hidden for convenience.

---

## Rule 5

Public interpretation should invite understanding, not dictate belief.
